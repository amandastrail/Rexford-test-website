What Is a Builder Finish Package?

Amanda Wiebe 5 min read

If you are pricing a new home or a large renovation, you will run into the phrase “builder finish package” early, usually in the same paragraph as a number that seems reasonable. It is one of the most misunderstood parts of a construction contract, and it is where a lot of budgets quietly come apart.

Here is a plain explanation of what it is, what it covers, and the questions worth asking before you sign anything.

The short version

A builder finish package is the list of everything the builder has priced for the visible, selectable parts of your home. Flooring, cabinets, countertops, tile, plumbing fixtures, lighting, hardware, paint, doors, trim, appliances in some cases.

The structure of the house is quoted from the drawings, because a joist is a joist. The finishes cannot be quoted that way, because your taste has not been decided yet. So the builder needs some assumption to price against, and the finish package is that assumption written down.

Allowances versus specifications

This is the distinction that matters most, and it is worth learning before your first pricing meeting.

A specification names the actual product. “Kohler Purist single-hole lavatory faucet, brushed nickel.” There is no guesswork. If that item is in the contract, that is what gets installed and that is what it costs.

An allowance is a dollar figure held in the budget for a category you have not chosen yet. “Plumbing fixture allowance: $9,000.” You go shopping later, and whatever you pick gets measured against that number. Come in under it and you get credited. Go over and the difference lands on a change order.

Most finish packages are a mix of both. The more specifications and the fewer allowances, the more your quoted price resembles your final price.

What a good package should tell you

A finish package worth trusting is specific about all of the following:

  • Product, model and finish for anything specified, not just a category name
  • Quantities, especially in tile and flooring, including waste factor
  • Whether the number is supply only or supply and install. This is the single most common source of a nasty surprise. A $12 per square foot tile allowance means very different things depending on whether it includes setting materials, labour and the Schluter.
  • Grade level. “Quartz countertops” covers a range of about three times in price. Which group of slabs are you actually being priced for?
  • Where the line is drawn. Are closet organizers in? Window coverings? Landscaping? Appliances? Mirrors and shower glass? Every one of those is commonly excluded and commonly assumed by the homeowner to be included.

If the package is one page and says things like “designer lighting package” with no dollar figure and no products, it is not a package. It is a placeholder.

Why builders use them at all

They are not a trick. They serve a real purpose.

They let a builder give you a price before you have made two hundred decisions. They keep the trades moving, because a standard cabinet line and a standard tile can be ordered on a predictable lead time. And they give the builder purchasing leverage, since buying the same products across many homes is cheaper than sourcing something new every time.

That last point is why the standard package usually is the best value in the whole quote. If the builder’s standard flooring works for you, take it. You will never buy it that well on your own.

Where homeowners get caught

Allowances set low to win the job. A quote is easier to accept if the finishes look affordable. If the lighting allowance is $2,500 for a 3,000 square foot home, that number was chosen to make the bid look good, not to light your house. Ask what the last three clients actually spent in that category.

Upgrades priced at retail plus markup. Some builders make their margin on upgrades. Ask how upgrade pricing is calculated before you fall in love with something.

Selection deadlines. Packages usually come with dates. Miss the tile deadline and the builder substitutes the standard product to keep the schedule, or charges you for the delay. Both are reasonable, and both surprise people.

Change order fees. Ask whether there is an administrative fee per change and what it is. Twenty changes at $250 each is a real number.

The last ten percent. Hardware, mirrors, closet shelving, exterior lighting, garage door style, address numbers. Small individually, several thousand dollars together, and rarely in the package.

How this looks under a cost-plus model

We work cost-plus, which changes the shape of this conversation.

Under a fixed-price contract, the finish package protects the builder’s margin. Anything you choose above the allowance is a change order, and the builder has an incentive to set allowances where they land in their favour.

Under cost-plus, you pay the actual invoiced cost of the materials plus an agreed fee, and you see the invoices. There is no upgrade markup to negotiate, because there is no gap between the allowance and the real cost for the builder to sit in. The allowance schedule stops being a pricing device and becomes what it should be: a budget you are tracking against, in the open, as you go.

That does not make the numbers softer. You still need realistic allowances, and we would rather set them honestly and have a hard conversation in month one than have a pleasant one now and an unpleasant one in month seven.

What to ask before you sign

  1. Which items are specified and which are allowances?
  2. Is each allowance supply only, or supply and install?
  3. What did your last few clients actually spend against these allowances?
  4. What is excluded entirely?
  5. How is upgrade pricing calculated, and is there a change order fee?
  6. When is each selection due?

A builder who answers all six clearly, in writing, is telling you something useful about how the rest of the project will go.
