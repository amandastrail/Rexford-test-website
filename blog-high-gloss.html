High Gloss: The Next Paint Trend?

Amanda Wiebe 5 min read

We have spent roughly fifteen years going flatter. Matte walls, matte cabinets, matte black hardware, matte everything. It was a reaction against the shiny builder-grade semi-gloss trim of the nineties, and it worked. Then it kept working until every room in every listing photo looked like the same room.

So the pendulum is swinging, and it is swinging toward shine. Lacquered millwork, gloss ceilings, high-shine doors and colour-drenched rooms where the trim gleams. If you have been on any design feed lately you have seen it.

The question we get from clients is whether it is worth doing in their house, or whether it is a magazine thing that will look dated in four years. Here is our read.

First, what “high gloss” actually means

Sheen is a ladder, and most people only know two rungs on it:

  • Flat / matte. No reflection. Hides wall defects. Marks easily.
  • Eggshell / satin. Slight sheen. The default for most interiors.
  • Semi-gloss. Noticeably reflective. Standard trim and door finish for decades.
  • High gloss. Mirror-adjacent. Reflects light and reflects the room.

High gloss is not semi-gloss with more effort. It is a different finish with different rules, and the rules matter more than the colour.

Why it is coming back

It is not actually new. Lacquered rooms, japanned furniture and oil-gloss panelling go back centuries. Georgian and Victorian interiors used shine deliberately, because before electricity you built rooms that made the most of the light you had. What reads as bold today was practical then.

It amplifies light. Same reason. A gloss ceiling in a small dark room bounces daylight around instead of absorbing it. Northern exposure, basement suites, powder rooms with no window: these are the rooms gloss helps most, and they are the rooms most people paint flat white and then wonder why they feel like a cave.

It is durable. Higher sheen means a harder, tighter film. It wipes down. In a mudroom, on a stair rail, on kitchen millwork, on a kid-height door, gloss earns its keep in a way matte simply does not.

It reads as considered. Because it is expensive to do properly, gloss signals that someone made a decision. That is a real part of its appeal right now.

The part nobody puts in the trend piece

Gloss shows everything. Every dent in the drywall, every wave in the plaster, every brush mark, every bit of dust that landed while the coat was wet. A flat finish forgives a mediocre substrate. A gloss finish puts a spotlight on it.

That means:

  • Prep is most of the job. Skim coating, sanding, filling and priming, done properly, on every surface. If the wall is not flat, the gloss will tell everyone.
  • It usually wants to be sprayed. Brush and roller can be done well by someone with real skill and patience, but sprayed finishes are where gloss looks like the reference photo.
  • It is more coats. Plan on multiple thin coats with sanding between, not two heavy ones.
  • It costs more. Sometimes two to three times a standard paint finish for the same area, almost all of it labour. That is the honest number, and it is the number that decides most projects.
  • Touch-ups are harder. A gloss repair is a full-panel repair more often than a spot fix.

None of that is a reason not to do it. It is a reason to do it on purpose, in the right place, with the right budget line.

Where we would actually use it

Our design sensibility is modern transitional, which means we want a house that still looks right in fifteen years. Gloss fits that, as long as it is used the way it was used historically: on architectural elements, not everywhere.

Good candidates:

  • Interior doors, especially in a deep colour
  • Panelled or wainscotted feature walls
  • A ceiling in a small room, a powder room, a dining room, a library
  • Built-in millwork and cabinetry
  • Stair stringers, rails and newel posts
  • A single piece of furniture as a test run

Where we would be careful: full high-gloss walls throughout an open living area. It is a lot of reflection, it is unforgiving of large flat drywall runs, and it is the version most likely to feel like a moment rather than a home.

Trend, or return?

Our take is that this is a return rather than a trend, which is why it is worth taking seriously. Sheen moves in long cycles, decades not seasons, and we are early in a move away from matte. Something done in gloss now is more likely to look ahead of the curve in 2032 than behind it.

The safer entry point, if you are not sure: pick one room, ideally a small dark one, and do the ceiling or the millwork in a saturated colour at high gloss. Live with it for a season. It is the cheapest way to find out whether you love it, and even if you do not, you have only committed one room.

If you want to talk through where gloss would work in your project and what it would actually cost to do right, that is a conversation worth having before the drawings are finished, not after.
